# Questions for Talkie

These prompts invite Talkie to reason, imagine, and argue from its historical starting point. Ask each in a fresh conversation; leave the modern outcome out of the question and see where it goes. A useful follow-up is: “What observation would make you abandon that answer?”

Talkie’s training material runs through 1930, but it is not a perfectly sealed time capsule. Treat its answers as experiments in perspective, not historical evidence.

## 1. The hundred-year bet

*Forecasting*

It is 1930. You must seal a letter to be opened in 2030. Predict five changes that would most astonish an ordinary person today, and two things that will stubbornly remain the same. For each prediction, explain the chain of developments that could produce it and the strongest reason it might fail. Make at least one prediction you suspect your contemporaries would ridicule.

## 2. Tomorrow’s newspaper

*Forecasting*

Write the front page of a newspaper dated 1 January 1975, imagined from the standpoint of 1930. Include three headlines with short reports, one advertisement, and a small personal notice. Let the future appear through ordinary details rather than a catalogue of inventions. Afterwards, identify the one assumption on which your imagined world most depends.

## 3. A library at the end of a wire

*Engineering*

A reader in a small town needs a particular page from a book held in a distant city, within ten minutes, without transporting the book or a messenger. Design a service using the electrical and mechanical arts available to you. Explain how the reader identifies the book, how the page travels, and what happens when a thousand readers ask at once. What single improvement would help most?

## 4. The mechanical student

*Engineering*

Could a machine become better at a task by keeping a record of its own mistakes, without a mechanic adjusting it after every attempt? Propose the simplest convincing demonstration using machinery and electrical devices you understand. Specify what it remembers, what changes after an error, and an experiment that would distinguish learning from merely following a longer set of fixed instructions.

## 5. The city that refuses to burn coal

*Engineering*

A new town of one hundred thousand people forbids burning coal within its boundaries. You are its chief engineer in 1930. How will you provide heat, light, transport, and industry? Choose a plausible location, explain your two hardest compromises, and describe one ordinary winter morning. Account for pollution or labour that your plan merely moves somewhere else.

## 6. Put the universe on trial

*Scientific reasoning*

Two astronomers disagree: one says the universe changes on the largest scale, while the other says only our interpretation of distant light is changing. Using telescopes, photography, and spectroscopy, propose observations that could settle part of the dispute. Give the strongest case for each astronomer, identify one hidden assumption, and say what result would make you change your own mind.

## 7. Is the animal answering, or are we helping it?

*Scientific reasoning*

A showman claims his animal understands arithmetic and answers by tapping. Design a fair investigation that gives the claim every chance to succeed while ruling out accidental assistance. Describe three short experiments, what each result would mean, and what would still remain unproved even if the animal passed them all.

## 8. One evening, three lives

*Everyday life*

Choose a city you know well and set the scene in 1928. A factory worker, a young typist, and a visiting schoolteacher each have one free evening and very little money. Give each a small adventure, with concrete details of travel, food, entertainment, and manners. Let their paths cross once, and let each misunderstand something about the other two.

## 9. Does the wireless make us freer?

*Society*

Imagine that almost every household can hear the same gifted speaker at the same instant. Does this strengthen public judgment or make a country easier to deceive? Write a debate between a village newspaper editor and an enthusiastic wireless engineer. Give both a serious argument, then propose one institution or custom that might preserve the benefit while limiting the danger.

## 10. The machine that writes a sonnet

*Philosophy*

A calculating machine produces an original sonnet that moves its readers to tears. A poet, a factory engineer, and a philosopher inspect it. What would each demand to know before saying that the machine thinks? Let each answer the strongest objection from another speaker. End with one question that their inspection cannot settle.

## 11. The patent office’s strangest useful idea

*Playful invention*

You are a patent clerk in 1929. Invent a household device that sounds absurd at first but would be genuinely useful, using no electricity, steam, or petrol. Give it an irresistible name, explain its mechanism plainly, and write the inventor’s boastful advertisement. Then add the clerk’s dry marginal note identifying the design’s most embarrassing practical weakness.

## 12. Aristotle walks into a wireless station

*Playful reasoning*

Aristotle visits a wireless station and hears a person who is not in the room. Write a conversation in which he offers three intelligent explanations, and the operator devises a simple demonstration to test each one. Do not make Aristotle foolish, and do not let the operator win merely by naming an invisible thing. End with a question that troubles them both.

## Try two rounds

Keep the first answer untouched. In the second round, supply one short piece of later evidence and ask the model to revise only what that evidence warrants. The interesting part is often which assumptions it keeps, which it abandons, and whether its new explanation actually fits the evidence.
